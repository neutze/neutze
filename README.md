# Johannes Neutze

**Engineering Manager in the merchant organization at DoorDash / Wolt. Munich.**

I lead a team that owns merchant-facing workflows. Right now that is a greenfield CRM I founded and hired from zero, and before it the order-operations workflows 150k+ merchants in 25+ countries run their business on. I came up through Android engineering, and I still pick the boring, reversible option most of the time — the rejected alternative is usually the interesting part of the story.

## High-scale platform reliability

I ran Zalando's mobile developer platform team: the CI/CD, release tooling, and developer-productivity systems every mobile team at the company built on, for apps with 20M+ monthly active users. The work that mattered was measurement before opinion — a developer survey to pick the targets, then Gradle Enterprise and XCMetrics to get real baselines. The first data showed iOS builds taking 18 minutes on CI against 4 on a laptop, and what it found underneath was machines running three threads on six available CPUs, on hardware two generations obsolete. That moved the argument from opinion to evidence.

Release engineering is where most of my judgment goes. A two-week release train on the client against continuous deployment on the backend; a 1% global canary ahead of full rollout; feature flags for per-market enablement; a documented rollback to last-known-good, which we exercised within 24 hours of a regression being declared a release blocker. One gate decides whether a build ships: a weekly reliability review, with a defined response below the line — inherit a team whose crash reporting has exhausted its own quota, and the first fix is a signal someone will act on, not more data.

I also ran a virtual 24x7 incident-responder pool for a whole mobile organization — roughly five Android and five iOS engineers from other teams rather than my five reports, restricted to senior engineers by written policy and oversubscribed, so I picked the rotation rather than filling it — and stood as Incident Commander through Cyber Week. The release freeze was inherited rather than mine; what I decided was how to ship under it — enforced in the build tooling, with a second stream admitting only risk-assessed changes, merged back without an outage.

## Systems architecture

The teams I lead work in Kotlin and Scala on Kafka, gRPC and REST, integrating with Salesforce and a merchant onboarding platform. What I bring to these systems is the architecture call. My hands-on depth started in mobile, and that is where the instinct for release risk and client-edge constraints comes from. The recurring one across four companies is **reuse over rebuild** — the incremental, reversible option instead of the impressive one, with the alternative named and costed:

- **The Tazz migration.** Tazz is a Romanian delivery company Wolt acquired. I led the engineering for moving its 5k+ merchants and 12k+ venues — commissions, contracts, banking and KYB — onto the platform after the acquisition. I routed it through the existing merchant onboarding service instead of writing a faster bulk importer. That cost throughput and kept every legal and compliance check running. It landed a week early with no production incidents raised, and the company's announcements channel named me as one of four people who drove the workstream to go-live. The ordering was the other half of that: data mapping, the compliance questions and the contract terms were all closed before implementation started, and the one legal question that did not resolve went into the plan as explicitly open rather than assumed. On a migration whose dominant failure mode is bad data, the sequence is the design.
- **The Tazz import, rerunnable before the first run.** I required the import be made rerunnable before it was ever run, tested that path, and used it for the second batch. I also sized ingestion to what the downstream services could absorb rather than to what the import could push, because pushing at source capacity is how a migration takes out the systems it is feeding.
- **The commission-sync connector.** I chose the Kafka platform the company already ran over standing up new infrastructure for it, and routed the output into the finance registry that already carried SOX compliance. Nothing new had to be procured, secured or operated, and the audit trail stayed in the system auditors already test. That was the property I actually wanted.
- **An engineering-owned boundary instead of vendor convenience.** On the same platform I held the architecture line against a vendor-first ownership model pushed for speed, keeping core business logic and integrations in systems we own. It is slower to build, and it is the only thing that keeps the vendor choice reversible. Closing it meant setting import-process ownership in writing and conceding a simplified wider design to keep the engineering non-negotiables — which is what let the feature ship instead of stall. The non-negotiables I held were a single engineering-owned adapter for every write, every field that adapter needs, and the vendor accountable for whether the person approving a change is entitled to.
- **How that boundary was actually won.** I could not win "full vendor versus not" in a workshop against the business, so I wrote the design document instead, did the depth work with the regulatory and finance functions, and recruited a product owner with standing on the business side to argue it with me. I had the technical reasoning; they had the organizational legitimacy a technical lead did not.
- **A rollback that changed the strategy, not just the code.** Two months into migrating a public-facing service off a framework that had lost security support, a stability incident forced a rollback mid-flight. Rather than push the same approach through again, I shifted the team to prove the new stack in a shadow environment against real production traffic — without it ever serving a request — before routing anything live through it. Slower, and it prevented the incidents a second blind attempt would have hit.
- **A sidecar instead of a rewrite.** Modernizing a legacy Java calendar app with hundreds of thousands of users, I moved features toward Kotlin alongside the old stack rather than freezing delivery for a rebuild.
- **A backend-for-frontend instead of waiting.** Through a two-brand merger I put a BFF layer between the rider app and upstream services, so mobile shipped against mock data and its own timeline rather than blocking on an upstream refactor — and aligned six codebases on shared patterns, so engineers could move between them without relearning the structure.
- **Native instead of cross-platform, in 2016.** Rebuilding the rider app, I distilled three predecessor apps into one native build rather than carrying on with the inherited Appcelerator Titanium stack. The cross-platform shortcut had already produced a maintainability trap in an acquired company's codebase, and not repeating it was the constraint — alongside low data consumption, because couriers were on prepaid plans.
- **Layered architecture, built once.** Presentation, domain use cases, data behind repositories, dependency injection scoped per feature — shared across the Android and iOS apps of a regulated digital-health product, so authentication, networking and persistence were built once rather than per screen.

## Engineering management and team topology

I start teams from scratch, inherit them mid-flight, and rebuild them when they stall. Two teams from zero to three — Selfapy's mobile team while still writing code, and Merchant CRM's engineering team, bootstrapped by redesigning the talent profile: splitting one unfillable combined role into separate Salesforce-platform and backend positions. That produced 75+ sourced candidates in week one, and a first final round within 7 days, against a pipeline that had moved nobody in four months. Contributed to 10+ hires across my time at Wolt, three of them roles I owned outright on the CRM team.

The other two shapes are on the same list. I took a newly acquired team on an interim basis while running the integration it came from, and protected its onboarding ramp under delivery pressure rather than pointing it straight at the roadmap. At Zalando I took a platform team from two engineers to five, through a trough of one. That second one is where I would put the retention answer, and it is not a number. I have no attrition rate worth quoting; what I have is who grew.

Every formal promotion case I have put forward has landed — a senior engineer to Staff, by closing the specific visibility and stakeholder-communication gap a prior cycle had flagged, and a mid-level DevOps engineer to senior in a job family too new to have a leveling rubric, which meant writing the calibration as well as the case. I have also mentored two successive leads of an adjacent team at my manager's request. It was part of the mandate I was hired into, and the second of them was a former report of mine who succeeded the first — so I coached the same engineer once as his manager and again as a peer running his own team. Mentoring and availability, not direction: they did not report to me and I did not set their roadmap.

The mechanisms I care about are the ones that outlast me:

- **Decision records as a habit.** An RFC and ADR practice plus a documented shortcuts register, so trade-offs stop being oral history and a deliberate short-term debt has a name, an owner and a fix.
- **A structural proposal executed on someone else's budget.** The combined engineering role on that program could not be filled, so I proposed splitting it into a platform track and a backend track. A hiring freeze landed in the same window and those two unfilled roles were the ones cut. Another organization then funded and staffed the platform half itself, at larger scale than I had asked for. The split I designed happened; it happened without me influencing it, and the cost was that the largest engineering group on the program sat outside my reporting line.
- **Delivery without a reporting line.** Leading a migration workstream as a virtual stream across four different relationships at once — one direct report, a peer at my own level, and two counterparts on the acquired company's side I unblocked rather than directed — in a program where thirteen workstreams shared dependencies. Holding one delivery together across four different levers is the part that was hard, not the headcount.
- **Governance built where it was missing, then around its own defect.** The CRM program had no decision-making structure, so I built one: steering and working layers with a published RACI, and decisions recorded in shared trackers rather than left in private messages. The model I built then turned out to have a defect of its own. It left engineering consulted-only on five of its six accountability rows, including the requirements for its own implementation work, so I built peer-level governance alongside it rather than waiting for the document to be rewritten. Reading a mis-designed accountability model as structural, rather than as a personality problem, is the part that took longest to learn.
- **Making the case for a platform function.** I wrote the case to my director for a merchant platform function — a costed team, a proposed scope, and the metrics I was willing to be judged against. A foundations team was later formed on the same rationale, with a backend-services scope rather than the mobile-platform one I proposed. I shaped what got built; I did not get what I asked for, and those are different claims.
- **Headcount cases, in the language of the people who allocate.** Both of those are instances of something I have done at every management role since 2017: prepare the headcount case — which roles, at which levels, against which work — and hand it up as the input my manager's plan gets built on. My manager presents it and makes the call. I have never owned a budget line, and preparing the case is not the same claim as approving it. What it does mean: I argue for capacity in the terms the people allocating it actually use.
- **A gate, not a checklist.** Delivering a merchant consent framework, the durable artifact was not the three consent categories or the revocation flows. It was routing every new third-party cookie through procurement with a data processing agreement. The driver was regulatory rather than a usability request — a tax-reporting directive plus marketing-consent law, where the real exposure is failing to deliver a mandatory notice to the correct legal contact. That gate constrains everyone who came after me, including people who never knew I set it.
- **Carrying the senior technical seat until it was filled.** On that same team I carried the architecture review myself and borrowed cross-team capacity for the rest, until a staff engineer finally joined. I reported upward that implementation quality and velocity both improved once they did, rather than claiming the gap had been costless.
- **Capacity argued in the open.** I won 30% of the year for technical fundamentals, and 15% for defects, by writing the deferred product work into the roadmap next to it. The same document allocated 0% to UX improvements, which is the part that made the split real — an allocation nothing visibly loses to is a wish written over an existing plan. The documented deferral is the mechanism — without it the split is just a number someone agreed to once.
- **Ownership designed into the process.** Rotational release management, and feature owners writing their own release notes, distribute responsibility structurally instead of routing it through me.
- **One name against each piece of work.** A subset of the team takes a problem and exactly one person is accountable for it, rather than me directing several engineers individually against the same piece of work. The reason I run it this way is that it is the part that scales: adding engineers adds parallel problems with named owners, where the direct-instruction model degrades as soon as the team is bigger than the manager's attention. It is also how a small team ends up with more surface than its headcount suggests.

## What I run at home

A small llama.cpp inference stack on an M2 Max, entirely on hardware I own — no cloud model APIs anywhere in it, every endpoint bound to `127.0.0.1`. The documents it reads are my own scanned records, so the locality is the requirement rather than a preference. It is personal-scale and I would not claim otherwise: four model servers, one machine, one user, no availability target. What it is good for is keeping the architecture muscle in use.

**The boundary is the part I would defend in a review.** One repo owns infrastructure: model files, ports, GPU layers, batch sizes, how much context the hardware can actually afford, all declared in a single `servers.toml`. A second owns the application: what to extract, which prompts, how to judge a result, what to write back. **The application never re-declares any of it — it asks the running server `GET /props` for the context window it actually has.** The obvious alternative is a config copy in the consumer, which is faster to write and correct exactly until the day it silently isn't. The cost of the choice is real and worth naming: those server keys and ports are now a published interface, so renaming one breaks the consumer at run time with nothing failing in the producer.

**Two models and a judge — the pipeline decision I like most is the one that made it slower.** For document metadata, two models extract every field independently and a third arbitrates — picks a winner, states a confidence — and only high-confidence results are written back. Everything else goes to a review queue, and the notes I add there get replayed through the arbiter, so correcting it reduces future corrections. One model with a confidence threshold would have been a third of the compute. On hardware I already own, that difference is wall-clock rather than budget, which is what made the trade affordable. It would also have been trusting a number that models are known to be bad at producing. **Disagreement between two independent runs is a better signal than either one's self-assessment.**

The honest misses: arbitration triples the inference cost per document — wall-clock and heat on hardware I own rather than a bill, since nothing in the path is metered — and that published interface has no contract test guarding it. I know, and I have not fixed it.
